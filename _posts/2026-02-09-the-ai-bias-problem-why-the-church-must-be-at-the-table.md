Here’s something that doesn’t come up enough in conversations about AI: it can be wrong in ways that matter deeply to the church’s mission.

Not wrong like a typo. Wrong like: generating images that erase certain ethnicities. Wrong like: misrepresenting religious traditions because they were underrepresented in the training data. Wrong like: reinforcing the exact biases we’ve been trying to dismantle for generations.

Think about something simple: if I say the word *shoe*, what comes to mind?

![Diverse array of athletic shoes showcased in vibrant colors and various styles.](/assets/uploads/2026/03/15475641-1024x576.jpeg)

A tennis shoe? A high heel? A boot? Maybe something else entirely. None of those answers are wrong—but they reveal something about your context, your experiences, and what you’ve been exposed to.

![close up photography of pair of silver leather platform stilettos](/assets/uploads/2026/03/plg3kc7cv0o-1024x678.jpg)

AI works the same way. Except instead of one person’s perspective, it’s drawing from massive datasets shaped by history, culture, and power. And those datasets are not neutral.

This is the AI bias problem — and it’s one of the most important reasons the church can’t afford to sit this conversation out.

## Where Bias Comes From

AI learns from training data — vast collections of text, images, and information scraped largely from the internet and existing databases. The problem is that those sources reflect the world as it has been, not as it should be. Historical inequities, underrepresentation of certain communities, and cultural blind spots all get baked into the model.

The result? An AI system that may:

- Misrepresent or flatten diverse religious and cultural traditions
- Generate images that default to certain races, body types, or aesthetics
- Fail to understand the nuances of communities it has little data about
- Inadvertently exclude the very people the church is called to serve

## This Is an Ethical Issue, Not Just a Technical One

For the church, AI bias isn’t a bug report. It’s a justice issue. The church is called to dignity, inclusion, and service to the marginalized. If the AI tools we’re adopting are reinforcing systems of exclusion, we have a responsibility to name that — and to push back.

We have a moral imperative, as communities built on the belief that every person bears the image of God, to demand that the technologies we use reflect that conviction.

## The Danger of Opting Out

Here’s the thing, though: stepping back from AI doesn’t protect the church or the people we serve. It just means the church’s voice is absent from the conversation shaping these tools.

AI systems are being developed right now, with or without input from faith communities. The more diverse voices — including churches that serve marginalized communities — are present in those conversations, the fairer and more transparent those systems can become.

Our participation brings something tech companies desperately need: a commitment to human flourishing as a core design principle, not an afterthought.

## Practical Steps for Your Church

- **Test your tools.** When you use AI to generate images for your church, look at what it produces. Does it reflect your actual community? If not, refine your prompts or push back on the output.
- **Ask who’s missing.** When AI gives you research or recommendations, ask: whose perspective is this missing? Who isn’t represented in this output?
- **Stay engaged.** Follow conversations about AI ethics in faith communities. Organizations like the Episcopal Church, the USCCB, and others are actively working through these questions.
- **Teach your congregation.** AI literacy is a form of digital discipleship. Help your people understand how these tools work — and where they fall short.

The church has always been at its best when it shows up at the table of the hard conversations. This one is no different.
