Most churches treat email like a digital bulletin — a weekly dump of announcements that goes out on Friday afternoon and gets ignored by Saturday morning. And then they wonder why nobody reads it.

Email is still one of the most effective communication tools you have. The people on your list chose to be there. They gave you permission to land in their inbox. That's a relationship, and it deserves more thought than a copy-paste of the weekend's announcements.

Here are the five emails that can actually move the needle — the ones most churches are either not sending at all, or sending so poorly they might as well not bother.

## 1. The first-time guest email

If someone fills out a connection card or registers as a new visitor on your website, they should receive an email within 24 hours. Not a form letter. Something warm, personal, and low-pressure.

This email should thank them for coming, tell them you'd love to see them again, and give them one clear next step — usually an invitation to come back next week or to check out a specific resource. No announcements. No event calendar. Just a genuine "we're glad you were here."

This is the single most underused email in church communications, and it has one of the highest potential impacts on first-time guest retention.

## 2. The new member / next steps email sequence

When someone decides to get more involved — they join a membership class, sign up for a small group, or indicate they want to serve — they should enter an automated email sequence that walks them through their next steps over several weeks.

This isn't spammy. It's actually serving people. Most people want to get more connected but don't know how to take the next step. An email sequence removes the friction and guides them forward at their own pace.

## 3. The pastoral letter

This one surprises people. Once a month, your lead pastor should send a personal email to the congregation. Not announcements. Not a sermon summary. Just a letter — something they're learning, a challenge they're sitting with, a passage they can't stop thinking about.

It builds intimacy at scale. People feel like they know their pastor in a way that Sunday mornings alone can't create. Open rates on these emails are almost always significantly higher than regular church newsletters.

## 4. The event follow-up

After every major church event — a conference, a baptism service, a community outreach — send an email to attendees within 48 hours. Share photos. Remind them of what God did. Give them a way to keep the momentum going.

Most churches work incredibly hard to pull off an event and then do absolutely nothing to follow up. The follow-up email is where the event continues to bear fruit.

## 5. The re-engagement email

Every church has people who used to be engaged and then quietly faded. Your email list probably has hundreds of people who haven't opened an email in months. Don't just keep sending them the same content and hoping they'll come back.

Send a simple, honest re-engagement email: "We've noticed we haven't heard from you in a while. We miss you. No pressure — just want you to know the door is always open." Then give them a real link to reconnect.

You'll lose some subscribers. That's fine. The ones who re-engage are worth it, and cleaning your list makes your other emails perform better anyway.

## Where to start

You don't need to build all five of these this week. Pick one. The first-time guest email is probably the highest-leverage starting point if you're not doing it yet. Set it up in whatever email tool you're using — SendFox, Mailchimp, whatever — and make it automatic.

Great church communication isn't about sending more emails. It's about sending the right ones, to the right people, at the right moment.
