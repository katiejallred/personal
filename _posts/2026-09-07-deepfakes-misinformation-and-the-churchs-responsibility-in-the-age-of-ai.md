A video surfaces showing a well-known Christian leader saying something they never said. It spreads through church WhatsApp groups before anyone thinks to fact-check it. By the time it's debunked, thousands of people have already formed an opinion based on a lie.

This isn't a hypothetical. It's happening. And the church — as a community built on truth — has a particular responsibility to respond well.

## What Are Deepfakes?

Deepfakes are AI-generated audio or video that realistically depicts someone saying or doing something they never actually said or did. The technology has advanced to the point where a convincing fake can be produced with minimal technical skill and minimal cost.

This isn't only a political problem. It's a church problem. Pastoral reputations, community trust, and theological authority are all vulnerable to synthetic media designed to deceive.

## Misinformation and the Believing Community

Research consistently shows that misinformation travels faster and farther than corrections. And communities built on shared belief — like churches — can be particularly susceptible, because we extend trust to our networks and to sources that align with our worldview.

The question isn't whether misinformation will reach your congregation. It will. The question is whether you've prepared them to recognize it.

## The Church's Responsibility

Proverbs 18:17 says, "The first to state his case seems right, until another comes and cross-examines him." That verse was written three thousand years ago and it perfectly describes social media. The church has a theological commitment to truth that should make us the most careful, most skeptical, most discerning consumers and sharers of information online.

That means teaching our congregations:

- **Pause before sharing.** If something outrages you, that's a signal to slow down — not speed up.
- **Check the source.** Who published this? What's their track record? Is there a byline?
- **Look for corroboration.** If only one outlet is reporting something, be skeptical.
- **Verify video.** Tools like InVID and Google's reverse image search can help identify manipulated media.
- **Correct publicly when wrong.** If you shared something that turned out to be false, say so. Publicly. Model integrity.

## Media Literacy as Discipleship

I've become convinced that media literacy is a form of modern discipleship. Teaching people how to think critically about what they consume online is part of forming them into people who love truth, pursue justice, and refuse to weaponize information against their neighbors.

The church that takes this seriously — that helps its congregation navigate a world full of synthetic media and manufactured outrage — is doing genuinely countercultural, gospel-shaped work.

Be that church.
