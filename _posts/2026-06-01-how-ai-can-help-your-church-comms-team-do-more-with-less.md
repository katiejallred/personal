If you're a one-person church communications department — or a volunteer who somehow ended up responsible for the website, social media, bulletin, and weekly email — this one's for you.

AI tools aren't going to replace you. But they can act like a very capable assistant who never gets tired, never needs a lunch break, and is available at 11pm on a Tuesday when you're trying to finish the weekend bulletin.

Here's how church communicators are actually using AI right now — practically, ethically, and in ways that genuinely save time without sacrificing authenticity.

## First drafts, not final drafts

The most useful thing AI can do for your communications workflow is kill the blank page problem. Starting from nothing is the hardest part of writing. Starting from a rough draft — even a mediocre one — is much easier.

Use AI to generate a first draft of your weekly email, a caption for an event post, or a series of announcement options. Then edit, adjust, and make it sound like your church. The final product should still be yours — AI just removes the friction of getting started.

One important note: your pastor's voice is irreplaceable. Don't try to use AI to replace their pastoral writing. Use it to support your own communications work, not to generate content that should feel personally authored by your leadership.

## Repurposing sermon content

Your pastor spent 15 to 20 hours preparing a sermon. That content shouldn't live and die in one Sunday morning service. AI can help you pull it apart into multiple pieces of content.

Take a sermon transcript (most transcription tools are free or cheap) and use AI to help you extract key quotes for social media, summarize the main points for an email recap, identify questions from the message that could become discussion questions for small groups, and draft a short devotional based on the central text.

That's four pieces of content from one sermon. For a one-person team, that's transformative.

## Brainstorming and ideation

Stuck on what to post? AI is an excellent brainstorming partner. Give it context — your church's values, the current sermon series, an upcoming event — and ask it to generate 20 post ideas. You won't use most of them. But buried in the list, there will be two or three that spark something real.

I use AI for this constantly in my own work. It's not that the ideas are brilliant — it's that having a list of options breaks me out of the mental loop of "I don't know what to create" and into the more manageable mode of "which of these is worth developing?"

## Editing and proofreading

Paste any piece of writing into an AI tool and ask it to check for clarity, grammar, and tone. Ask it to flag anything that might be confusing to someone unfamiliar with church culture. This is especially useful for outreach-facing content — it's easy to slip into insider language without realizing it.

Ask AI: "Read this as if you've never attended church. What's confusing or off-putting?" The feedback can be genuinely eye-opening.

## What AI can't do

It's worth being honest about the limits. AI cannot know your congregation. It can't capture the moment when your worship team had that spontaneous extended song last Sunday and half the room was in tears. It can't tell the story of the family who got baptized last month with the specificity and love that only someone who knows them can bring.

The most powerful church communications will always be rooted in real stories, real people, and real relationships. AI can help you communicate those stories more efficiently, but it can't generate them. That's still your job — and it's the most important part of the job.

## Getting started this week

If you've never used AI tools in your communications workflow, start simple. Take your next weekly email announcement and write a rough version yourself, then paste it into ChatGPT or Claude and ask: "Can you make this clearer and more inviting without changing the core information?"

Compare what comes back to what you wrote. Notice what it changed and why. Edit the result until it sounds like you again.

That's the whole workflow, right there. It gets faster and more intuitive the more you use it. And for a one-person team trying to communicate on behalf of an entire church, that kind of leverage matters.
