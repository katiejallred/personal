before you start

Slow down before you speed up.

It's your first week. Every ministry leader has stopped by your desk with something that needs to be promoted. You don't know where the logo files are. You're not sure which decisions are yours to make. And Sunday keeps coming.

Take a breath. Your first ninety days aren't about output. They're about learning.

You'll be tempted to come in fast, change everything, and prove yourself in the first few weeks. Resist it. The most respected communicators I know spent their first months listening, learning, and earning trust, and then built something remarkable on that foundation.

I've sat in your chair. I managed web, email, and social for one of Nashville's largest multi-campus churches, and I've spent the years since teaching church communicators, writing the textbook on church communications, and helping churches of every size. This workbook is the plan I'd hand you on day one.

"For which of you, wanting to build a tower, doesn't first sit down and calculate the cost…?" (Luke 14:28)

Ninety days of listening is how you count the cost. Here's the road ahead:

How to use this workbook

  • Print it and write all over it. Keep it on your desk. The blank lines are for you.
  • Go in order. Each phase builds on the one before it. Skipping the audit is how new directors end up redesigning things that were working.
  • Behind already? That's okay. Start wherever you are and keep moving.

Phase 1 · Days 1–14

Listen and audit.

Before you change anything, understand everything. A communication audit is simply an honest inventory: what exists, who runs it, and whether it's doing its job.

Your goal: by day 14, you can name every channel your church uses, who has access to it, and one thing that's working or confusing about each.

Phase 1 · Days 1–14

Interview your people.

Your pastor, your ministry leaders, and your members already know where communication breaks down. They just don't usually say so unless someone asks. Schedule short conversations, take notes, and mostly listen.

Who to talk to

  • Lead pastor
  • Executive or administrative pastor
  • Every ministry leader who sends you requests
  • Your predecessor, if you can
  • A few key volunteers
  • Someone who started attending this year
  • A long-time member
  • The front office (they hear everything)

Questions to ask

  1. How do you usually find out what's happening at church?
  2. What's working in how we communicate right now?
  3. What feels cluttered or confusing?
  4. What do you wish the communications team did differently?
  5. What's one thing I should be careful not to change?
  6. If you could fix one thing tomorrow, what would it be?
Ask your pastor this one: "A year from now, what would make you say communications is going really well?" Their answer tells you what a win looks like, and buy-in from your lead pastor comes before any strategy.

Phase 2 · Days 15–30

Find the keys.

Where are the logo files? What are the approved colors? Who has the password to the church Instagram? This sounds boring. It's crucial. You can't build consistent communications until you know what the brand actually looks like, and you'd be surprised how often these files are scattered across personal computers and old shared folders, or don't exist at all.

Brand kit checklist

  • Logo: full color, one color, and white
  • Logo as a vector file (SVG, AI, or EPS)
  • Ministry and campus logos
  • Brand colors (with hex codes)
  • Fonts, and where they're licensed
  • Photo library, with permission to use
  • Templates for slides, social, and print
  • A style guide, even a one-page one

Phase 2 · Days 15–30

Learn the weekly rhythm.

Phase 3 · Days 31–60

Ask your people.

Your church members have opinions about how well the church communicates. A simple survey surfaces them. Keep it short, five to seven questions, so people actually finish it. Send it by email, link it on social, and put a card and a QR code in the seats on a Sunday.

Survey questions to start from

  1. How do you prefer to hear from the church? (email, text, app, social, bulletin, announcements from the stage)
  2. Where do you go first when you want to know what's happening?
  3. In the last month, did you miss something you would have wanted to attend? How did you find out about it, if you did?
  4. What do we communicate well?
  5. What feels cluttered or confusing?
  6. If you invited a friend, where would you send them first?
  7. Anything else you want the communications team to know?

Phase 3 · Days 31–60

Bring solutions, not just judgments.

By now you've seen a lot, and some of it probably made you wince. Here's the difference between a critic and a leader: a critic points out the problem. A leader brings the problem, a proposed fix, and a reason it matters. Sit down with your pastor and walk through what you found.

My findings, in one page

What I foundWhat I recommendWhy it matters

Agree on these before you leave the room

  • Our top three communication priorities for the rest of the year
  • Which decisions are mine to make, and which need approval
  • How ministries will send requests from now on (one door, not five)
  • How much notice a request needs: ________ weeks
  • What gets announced from the stage, and who decides
About boundaries: a request process protects your time and makes every ministry's work better. You'll explain the why over and over. That's normal. Say it kindly, and keep saying it.

Phase 4 · Days 61–90

Build the calendar.

A communications calendar is the tool that turns reactive scrambling into proactive strategy. Start with the big moments of the year and work backward. How many weeks of promotion does each one need? Put it on the calendar. What gets scheduled gets done.

one more thing

This is a marathon, not a race.

Your work will often be unseen and rarely thanked. I won't pretend otherwise. But you are a storyteller on a mission. Jesus said, "The harvest is abundant, but the workers are few" (Matt 9:37), and you are one of those workers.

My prayer for you, church communicator, is that you'd have the patience to listen before you build, the courage to say no so you can say yes to what matters most, and joy in the work, even on the hardest Monday. Don't burn out. This job needs you for the long haul.
